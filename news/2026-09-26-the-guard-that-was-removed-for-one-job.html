The Guard That Was Removed for One Job and Never Went Back

Machinery Safety September 26, 2026

Machine injuries are rarely mysterious. Investigate them and the same sentence keeps appearing: the guard was off. Sometimes it was removed for maintenance and not refitted. Sometimes it was defeated because it slowed the work. Sometimes the machine arrived second-hand and never had one.

What guarding is supposed to do

Guarding keeps people away from the parts that can injure them: rotating shafts, belts and pulleys, gears, cutters, nip points between rollers, and anything that moves under power. The right type depends on how often the operator needs access:

  • Fixed guards for parts that need no routine access. Bolted, not clipped, so removing one takes a tool and a decision.
  • Interlocked guards where access is regular. Opening the guard stops the machine, and on machines with run-down time, the guard stays locked until movement has stopped.
  • Adjustable or self-adjusting guards for tools where the material size varies, such as saws.
  • Trip devices — light curtains, pressure mats, two-hand controls — where a physical barrier would make the work impossible.

Why interlocks get bypassed

A defeated interlock is almost always a symptom rather than a crime. The usual causes are a guard that has to be opened many times an hour for a task nobody designed around, a machine that takes too long to restart, a jam that can only be cleared by watching the mechanism run, or a guard that no longer aligns and trips out constantly.

Treating this as a discipline problem alone means the tape comes off the switch and goes back on a week later. Ask instead what the operator was trying to achieve and redesign that: a hatch for clearing jams, a jog control at safe speed, a faster restart sequence, or a guard repaired so it stops nuisance-tripping.

Checking guarding is still real

  • Every moving part that can be reached is covered, including at the back and underneath where people rarely look.
  • Fixed guards are complete and properly fastened, not held with one bolt out of four or a cable tie.
  • Every interlock is function tested: open the guard and confirm the machine actually stops.
  • No temporary defeat remains — no tape, wedge, magnet or spare key left in a switch.
  • Emergency stops are reachable from the operating position and are tested, not assumed.

After maintenance is the moment of risk

The highest-risk time for any machine is the first run after work on it. Make refitting and function testing every guard a required step before handover, signed off by someone other than the person who did the repair. That single control catches most of the guards that would otherwise stay off for months.
