Heat Shows an Electrical Fault Long Before Smoke Does

Inspection & Monitoring September 24, 2026

Most electrical fires in commercial and industrial buildings do not start with a dramatic failure. They start with a connection that has been slightly loose for months. Resistance rises, the joint warms, the warmth accelerates oxidation, resistance rises again, and the cycle continues until something finally ignites. Throughout all of it, the panel looks entirely normal from the outside.

What an infrared survey sees

A thermal camera makes that invisible process visible. Surveyed under load, a healthy circuit shows an even temperature across its terminations. A developing fault shows as a hot spot: one terminal noticeably warmer than its neighbours, one phase warmer than the other two, or a breaker running hotter than identical breakers beside it.

The common findings are consistent across sites:

  • Loose or under-torqued terminations, by far the most frequent, often on circuits that were disturbed during a modification.
  • Unbalanced loads across phases, overloading one conductor while the others sit lightly loaded.
  • Failing breakers and contactors, where internal contacts have degraded and the device warms under normal current.
  • Corroded or dirty connections, common in dusty, humid or coastal environments.
  • Undersized or damaged cable feeding equipment that has grown over the years.

Doing it properly

A survey is only meaningful under realistic load, because a fault that shows at full current can be invisible at a quarter of it. Covers have to be removed by a competent person so the camera sees the terminations rather than the cabinet door, and that work is live work, which means proper planning, arc flash risk assessment, correct protective equipment and a second person present.

The report matters too. Each finding should record the temperature of the hot spot, the temperature of a comparable reference point, the load at the time, an image, and a priority reflecting how far above normal the rise is. Without the reference and the load, a temperature on its own means very little.

Where it pays off

Thermography is at its most valuable on the things that would hurt most if they failed without warning: main switchboards and incoming supplies, generator and changeover panels, motor control centres, distribution boards feeding critical plant, and the cooling systems in server rooms. An annual survey on those, with findings tracked year to year, turns a category of sudden failure into planned maintenance.
