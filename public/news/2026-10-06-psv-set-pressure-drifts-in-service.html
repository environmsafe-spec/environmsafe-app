A Safety Valve's Set Pressure Drifts, and Nothing Tells You

PSV Testing October 6, 2026

A pressure safety valve is set once, tagged, and then expected to hold that setting for years in an environment designed to attack it. Heat relaxes the spring. Condensate corrodes the seat. Process solids cut a path across the sealing face. Every one of those changes the pressure at which the valve lifts, and not one of them announces itself.

What drift looks like

A valve that has drifted upward opens late. The vessel sees a pressure it was never designed to hold before the valve relieves, which is the scenario the valve exists to prevent. A valve that has drifted downward opens early, which sounds harmless and is not: it vents product, it cycles, and each cycle hammers the seat and accelerates the damage that caused the drift. Operators learn to live with a valve that weeps, and a weeping valve is one that has already stopped protecting at its stamped pressure.

Why a visual inspection cannot find it

You can see corrosion on a body. You can see a broken tag or a missing seal. You cannot see what pressure a spring will lift at by looking at it, and that number is the entire function of the device. A valve can look immaculate and lift twenty percent high. A valve can look neglected and still be within tolerance. The only way to know is to put it under controlled, measured pressure and watch what it actually does.

What the bench tells you that nothing else can

  • Set pressure, measured. The pressure at which it actually lifts, against the pressure stamped on the plate.
  • Blowdown. How far the pressure must fall before it reseats. Too wide and you lose product on every lift; too narrow and it chatters.
  • Seat tightness to API 527. Whether it holds below set pressure, or has been quietly leaking.
  • A certificate with traceable results, which is what an insurer or a regulator will ask for.

The interval is not the point

API 576 sets inspection intervals from operating history and service severity, and a clean valve in clean service can safely go longer than a fouled valve in dirty service. But an interval is a plan, not evidence. The question that matters is not when the valve is next due — it is what the last bench test said, and whether anyone has one on file.

If the answer is that nobody knows, the valve is not a safety device. It is an assumption with a tag on it.
