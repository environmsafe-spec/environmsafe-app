Lifting Gear Fails at the Link Nobody Looked At

Inspection & Monitoring October 27, 2026

When a lift goes wrong, attention goes straight to the crane. In practice the crane is usually the best-maintained object in the chain. The load travels through a sling, a shackle, a hook and an anchor point, and those items are cheap, portable, easy to replace informally and inspected far less often than the machine lifting them.

The chain is only as good as its least-documented part

A certified crane with an uncertified sling is an uncertified lift. Every item carrying load needs an identity — a marking, a rating and a record — and an item with no identity cannot be proven fit for anything. In practice the unmarked sling in the back of the pickup is the one that gets used when the right one is in another store.

What takes gear out of service

  • Webbing slings: cuts, abrasion, heat glazing, chemical staining, and any damage that exposes the core yarn. A missing or illegible label is itself a reason to withdraw it.
  • Chain slings: stretch, gouges, twisted or bent links, and wear at the bearing points where links meet.
  • Wire rope: broken wires, birdcaging, crushing, and corrosion that has moved from surface discolouration into pitting.
  • Shackles and hooks: deformation, a widened throat, thread damage, and the substituted pin that is not the pin that came with the shackle.
  • Eyebolts and anchor points: side loading, which drops capacity dramatically and is almost never calculated at the point of use.

The arithmetic people skip

Sling capacity falls as the angle between legs widens. At sixty degrees each leg carries more than half the load; at one hundred and twenty it carries the whole of it. Crews who know this in a classroom still rig shallow angles in a yard because the shackle is where it is and the load has to move now. The tag on the sling states its rating at a stated configuration, not in every configuration.

A register, not a pile

Lifting accessories need a register: every item identified, rated, inspected on a stated interval, and formally withdrawn when it fails. Gear that is merely thrown away quietly tends to reappear. The register is also the document an insurer or client auditor asks for after an incident, and the answer cannot be assembled afterwards.
