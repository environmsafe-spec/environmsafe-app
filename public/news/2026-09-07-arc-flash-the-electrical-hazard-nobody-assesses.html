Arc Flash: The Electrical Hazard Most Facilities Never Assess

Electrical Safety September 7, 2026

Electrical incidents divide cleanly into two kinds. There is electric shock, which everyone anticipates and guards against. And there is arc flash, which most facilities outside heavy industry have never assessed at all, and which is the one that causes the catastrophic injuries.

Arc Flash Is a Different Hazard Entirely

An arc flash is not a shock. It is an explosive release of energy through the air between conductors, producing temperatures several times hotter than the surface of the sun, a pressure wave that can throw a worker across a room, and molten metal spray. The person does not need to touch anything. Standard workwear ignites. The injuries are burns and blast trauma rather than the cardiac effects associated with shock, and the treatment burden is enormous.

The Conditions That Create It Are Ordinary

Racking a breaker in or out, opening a panel to take a reading, a dropped tool bridging two terminals, a loose connection that has been heating quietly for months, insulation degraded by heat and dust, or a rodent in a switchboard. None of these are exotic. All of them are routine features of facilities operating in hot, dusty conditions with intermittent supply, which describes a great deal of industrial and NGO infrastructure across Yemen and the wider region.

What an Electrical Safety Assessment Establishes

  • Incident energy at each work location — how much energy an arc would actually release there, which determines everything downstream.
  • Approach boundaries and PPE categories — matched to that calculated energy rather than assumed from a general rule.
  • Labelling — so the person opening a panel knows the hazard before they open it, not after.
  • Protection coordination — because how fast upstream protection clears a fault directly sets how severe the arc is.
  • Safe work practices — de-energised working as the default, with live work permitted only where genuinely unavoidable and formally justified.

The Default Nobody Enforces

Almost every electrical safety standard says the same thing: work de-energised unless doing so introduces a greater hazard. In practice, live work happens constantly for a much weaker reason, which is that shutting down is inconvenient. That trade is made informally, by whoever is under time pressure, usually without anyone senior knowing it was made at all. Making the decision formal and visible is one of the highest-value changes available, and it costs nothing to implement.

Testing and inspection close the loop. Thermographic surveys find the loose, heating connections that become tomorrow's fault while they are still just warm. Insulation resistance testing catches degradation before it becomes a failure.
