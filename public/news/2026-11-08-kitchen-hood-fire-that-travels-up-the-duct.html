The Kitchen Fire That Travels Up the Duct

Fire Protection November 8, 2026

Most kitchen fires start small: oil in a pan overheats, a burner flares, someone reaches for a lid. The fire that destroys a building is usually a different one. It starts the same way, but the flame is drawn up into the hood, finds months of grease lining the duct, and keeps going somewhere nobody can reach with an extinguisher. Compound kitchens, guesthouses, staff canteens and food manufacturing lines all carry this risk, and it grows quietly between cleans.

Where the fire actually goes

Cooking vapour carries fat. As it cools inside the hood, filters and ductwork, it condenses into a sticky film that hardens and thickens over time. The extract fan keeps pulling hot air and flame into that space. A duct fire burns inside a closed metal channel that often runs through ceiling voids, shafts and walls, so it can spread to rooms far from the kitchen before anyone sees smoke.

What the standards expect

The reference for commercial kitchen exhaust is NFPA 96, the Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations. Fixed suppression over cooking appliances is normally a wet chemical system designed to NFPA 17A and listed to UL 300. Together they set out a few points that are not negotiable:

  • Exhaust systems inspected for grease build-up on a schedule set by cooking volume, from monthly for solid-fuel cooking to quarterly for high-volume operations such as 24-hour kitchens or charbroiling, and cleaned whenever deposits are found.
  • The suppression system inspected and maintained by a trained person at least every six months, with fusible links replaced on that cycle.
  • Activation that automatically shuts off fuel or power to the cooking appliances beneath the hood.
  • A manual release located on the route people use to leave the kitchen.
  • A Class K portable extinguisher near the cooking line, for use after the fixed system has discharged.

The details that fail in practice

Systems rarely fail because nobody installed them. They fail because the kitchen changed. A fryer is moved to a spot no nozzle covers. A wok burner is added to a line designed for flat griddles. Filters are left out after washing, or cleaning stops at the visible hood while the duct behind it is never opened. In LPG-fired kitchens, the gas shut-off valve may have been bypassed after a nuisance trip, so the suppression system discharges while the burners keep feeding the fire. And staff who have never been told otherwise still throw water on burning oil, which turns it into a fireball.

What an evaluation looks at

Our NFPA CFPS certified fire protection specialists review the kitchen as one system: appliance layout against nozzle coverage, the condition of the hood and duct, cleaning records, the suppression system and its maintenance history, the gas or electrical interlock, and the LPG supply feeding the cooking line. The result is a clear list of what is compliant, what needs correction, and in what order. Certified fire protection can also reduce insurance costs by up to 85%, and a kitchen that is properly protected and documented is part of that case.
