Nobody Notices Losing Their Hearing Until It Is Gone

Safety Evaluation October 10, 2026

Of all the injuries an industrial site produces, noise-induced hearing loss is the one that never announces itself. There is no moment of injury, no pain, no incident to report. The damage accumulates across years, and the first thing most people notice is struggling to follow a conversation in a busy room — long after the loss has become permanent.

The intuition that misleads people

Noise exposure is logarithmic, which means ordinary reasoning about it is wrong. An increase of three decibels doubles the sound energy reaching the ear. So eight hours at 85 dB and four hours at 88 dB and two hours at 91 dB all deliver the same dose. A worker who spends twenty minutes beside a compressor at 100 dB has taken a serious bite out of their daily allowance, and will tell you they were barely there.

This is why "it is only loud sometimes" is not reassurance. Brief, intense exposures matter as much as sustained moderate ones, and they are the ones nobody logs.

What a survey needs to do

  • Measure where ears are, not in the middle of the room. Noise falls off sharply with distance, and the figure that matters is at the operator's head.
  • Cover the full shift, including the loud minutes. A spot reading during steady running misses the blowdown, the compressor start, the drop test.
  • Produce exposure per person, not per area, because people move through a site and their dose is the sum of where they have been.
  • Identify what is actually loud. Usually a small number of sources dominate, and treating those is cheaper than issuing protection to everyone.

Why protection is the last control, not the first

Hearing protection is where most sites start, and it is the weakest option. It only works when worn correctly for the entire exposure, it is routinely removed for conversations, and its rated attenuation assumes a fit that real use rarely achieves. An enclosure around the noisy compressor protects everyone, all the time, without depending on anyone's compliance.

The point of a noise survey is not to prove earplugs are needed. It is to find the few sources that create the problem, so that most of it can be engineered out and protection is left covering what remains.
