Boiler Inspection: What Is Actually Being Checked

Boiler Safety September 8, 2026

Every boiler in commercial service carries an inspection certificate. Far fewer carry an honest account of what was actually examined, and the distance between those two things is where boiler incidents live.

A certificate is a record, not a guarantee

An inspection is a snapshot of a pressure system on one day, under one set of operating conditions. It records what a competent person found at that moment. It does not promise that nothing has changed since, and it says nothing at all about the parts nobody opened.

The most common problem we find is not a missing certificate. It is a certificate whose scope is narrower than the operator believes it to be. Someone signed for an external examination; the plant manager has been reading it as a clean bill of health for the whole system.

What a full examination covers

  • External examination of shell, seams, mountings, insulation, supports and foundations, looking for distortion, corrosion, leakage and the discoloration that follows overheating.
  • Internal examination of waterside and fireside surfaces: scale and deposit build-up, pitting, and cracking around stays, tube ends and other points of restraint.
  • Mountings and fittings, including water level gauges and controls, feed check valves, blowdown arrangements, and pressure gauges compared against a calibrated reference rather than against each other.
  • Safety valves, with set pressure verified on a certified bench rather than assumed from the previous certificate. This is the boiler's last line of defence and the one component that must never be taken on trust.
  • Controls and interlocks, particularly low water cut-outs and flame supervision, proven by function test and not by inspecting the wiring.

The failures that do not announce themselves

Scale only a few millimetres thick on a waterside surface insulates the metal from the water that is meant to cool it. The tube keeps taking heat from the fire and stops giving it up, and the metal runs hotter than it was designed to run. Nothing sounds wrong, the pressure gauge reads normal, and fuel consumption creeps up so gradually that nobody connects it to anything. The failure, when it arrives, arrives suddenly.

The same is true of a low water cut-out that has never been function tested. It looks identical whether it works or not.

What to ask for

Ask for the scope in writing before the inspection, not the certificate afterwards. A written scope tells you which examinations are included, which mountings will be removed, whether safety valves are going to a bench, and which tests are functional rather than visual. It also tells you what is not covered, which is the part worth reading twice.

Then ask what the inspector found, not merely whether the boiler passed. The findings are the value. The certificate is only the receipt.
